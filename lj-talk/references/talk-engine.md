# Talk argument guidance

Use this reference when the requested deck needs a scientific argument or teaching sequence. The
engine plan remains the build contract; this reference does not impose a fixed page count or language.

## Per-page movement

For each substantive page, record the audience state before it, the evidence or task it presents, and
the state the audience should carry forward. Remove pages with no change, except intentionally quiet
title, transition, activity, acknowledgement, or reference pages. A question may intentionally defer
its answer; do not reveal it early merely to make a self-contained slide.

## Global topology and cross-boundary handoffs

Before drafting individual slides for a multi-part mechanism or argument, map the **global topology
(simplest complete system / Epitome)** showing how sub-parts connect and explicitly type each major
edge: **causal** (physical mechanism), **transform** (measurement/calibration), **part–whole**
(single sample inside a full map/dataset), or **aggregation** (many samples tallied into a summary
distribution). Revisit the whole at opening, major handoffs, and closing so the audience never loses
sight of where a detail sits in the full picture.

At every major section or representation boundary, state a compact 5-field handoff in the engineplan:
`From → To (edge type)` · `Surviving landmark / mapping rule` · `Quantity domain & invariants` ·
`Transition choice (matched static anchor / zoom / clean cut)` · `Question the audience can now
answer`. Do not hide two different edge types (such as part–whole and aggregation) in a single
unexplained slide jump. Keep step granularity matched to audience prior knowledge (fade already
mastered steps to avoid expertise reversal; keep integrative landmarks). In PPTX delivery, ensure
every boundary reads clearly across **two consecutive static stills** using matched landmark
coordinates and explicit callouts; treat PowerPoint Morph as optional polish, never as a prerequisite
for comprehension.

## Teaching sequence

For a teaching deck, arrange a concept so students first encounter a concrete observation, decision,
or experience that makes the next question meaningful; then use evidence to develop the abstraction.
Keep a familiar case or object in view when it reduces needless context switching. When returning to it,
advance the students' explanation or decision rather than retelling it.

### Continuity between sessions

For a continuing lecture, begin with a brief retrieval or recap of the essential ideas students actually
learned in the preceding session and need here. Reuse familiar, approved images or questions where they
help retrieve that shared experience, then bridge naturally into this session through the unresolved
problem or application it enables. Choose the scale to suit the lesson; do not prescribe a universal
duration or reteach the whole preceding lecture. Do not treat material merely present in a later deck as
prior knowledge, and do not leak answers to a new prediction or activity. Omit or adapt this move for a
first lesson, when the brief explicitly skips it, or for a procedural one-page update where it has no job.

Reveal a system, mechanism, or result in states whose prerequisites are already established. Preserve a
prediction, comparison, or study-design opportunity until students have had a chance to make it. A
state should normally support one substantive cognitive move, but a quiet text page, table, recap, or
full-system view is valid when it has a clear job. A complete infographic may accumulate across the
deck or arrive near the end, depending on what students already know.

For a required activity, make the learner path work on the delivered pages: preparation or visible
evidence, a prompt, a real opportunity to respond, feedback or answer checking, and the next use of
that result. Keep only the parts that the activity needs; a question does not need a formulaic sequence
when it is rhetorical or deliberately held open. Put the student-facing action and evidence on canvas,
not solely in notes.

At a key transition, identify what the preceding observation lets students explain and what it leaves
unexplained, then make the next concept necessary to resolve that gap. Nearby topics are not therefore
prerequisites; do not treat a before/after sequence as evidence that the learner can make the connection.

Before discussing uncertainty or limits, state what the evidence contributes. Do not invent historical
necessity, simplify a result beyond its claim, or manufacture drama. Keep optional evidence and rich
lecturer notes available without making the student route carry all of that detail. Where relevant,
bridge the lecture to the actual lab or project by naming the next authentic decision students can
make; do not invent an assessment or force a descriptive or comparative project into a causal
experiment.

## Common arcs

- **Conference:** question, stakes, minimum study trust, strongest result, interpretation and
  alternatives, consequence, conclusion. Add only likely high-cost backup questions.
- **Lecture:** learning aim, prior knowledge, concept, prediction or retrieval, example, complication,
  application, synthesis. Preserve pauses, demonstrations, and reveal timing.
- **Defense:** question and gap, system/data, decision-relevant methods, results including
  complications, synthesis, limits, future work, conclusion.

Use message titles for evidence, data, and application pages where the claim can be supported. A title,
concept, setup, activity, acknowledgement, or reference page may instead use a descriptive label.

## Scientific traceability

For every numerical or factual scientific claim, record its source plus a useful locator such as a
figure, table, page, data column, or analysis output. Keep observation, transformation, and
interpretation distinguishable. A display band mapping, stretch, overlay, annotation, uncertainty,
sample size, and attribution belong in the relevant page fields or notes when they affect meaning.

Select backup pages by plausible audience questions: confounding, sensitivity, sample adequacy,
alternative mechanism, scope, or decision impact. They answer a question rather than merely storing
extra material.
